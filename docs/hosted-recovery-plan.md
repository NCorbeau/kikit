# Hosted recovery plan

Prepared on 2026-10-07 for [MAC-102](https://linear.app/mglownia/issue/MAC-102). This is a reviewed proposal and read-only provider investigation, not a configured schedule or a completed restore drill. The accepted [data policy](data-policy.md) targets daily backups, six-day retention, at most 24 hours of server-data loss while backups succeed, and restoration within four hours **after recovery starts**. Keep Hobby, Amsterdam, the $5/month Kikit usage target and the $20 workspace compute hard limit.

## Current evidence and provider boundary

Railway CLI 5.63.1 and the live GraphQL schema were inspected without invoking a mutation. Metadata queries found one attached PostgreSQL volume, 5,000 MB capacity and approximately 164.83 MB used, in Amsterdam. There were no existing volume backups and no backup schedules. Usage and limit observations are in an ignored local operational worksheet; project/workspace identifiers and credentials do not belong in this document. Measurements are a dated observation, not a growth forecast.

Railway's daily schedule runs every 24 hours and retains backups for six days. Manual backups require used space below half the volume capacity. Backups are incremental, charged for exclusive stored data; displayed sizes may lag by hours. Wiping a volume destroys its backups. Restoring stages a replacement volume at the original service, and applying the change redeploys that service. The old volume is retained, including its newer backups. Restores remain within the original project/environment. [Backup reference](https://docs.railway.com/volumes/backups).

The current `volumeInstanceBackupRestore` API takes the source volume-instance and backup IDs; it has no independent destination-service argument. Service duplication's deprecated API does not establish that backup history is copied. Environment duplication must not be treated as proof that a specific backup was restored. PITR explicitly creates a sibling service but adds another archive, retention and cost model; it is outside this daily-snapshot proposal. [PostgreSQL recovery options](https://docs.railway.com/guides/postgres-backups-restores), [PITR](https://docs.railway.com/volumes/point-in-time-recovery).

Therefore, do not restore a production volume as an isolated drill merely by clicking Restore and Deploy. The proposed handoff below first needs proof on disposable services. If Railway cannot preserve the source while handing the staged restored volume to a separate service, stop and obtain a supported isolated-restore procedure. An isolated logical `pg_dump`/`pg_restore` drill is useful evidence, but it does not prove recovery from the production daily volume backup.

## Cost review before enabling resources

Use current project/workspace usage, actual backup sizes and replica limits immediately before execution. Railway charges volumes/backups at $0.15 per GB-month, memory at $10 per GB-month and CPU at $20 per vCPU-month. Private database traffic avoids public egress charges. The $5 Hobby allowance belongs to the entire workspace; unused project headroom does not mean new resources are free. [Pricing](https://docs.railway.com/pricing/plans), [cost controls](https://docs.railway.com/pricing/cost-control).

For a sensitivity estimate, six independent complete replacements of the observed 0.165 GB footprint would retain about 0.99 GB, roughly $0.15/month. Real incremental sharing can reduce this; growth and WAL churn can increase it. At the existing 5 GB capacity, six full replacements would instead be about $4.50/month before manual backups. There is no verified per-volume backup dollar cap. Never present the small current-footprint estimate as an enforced recurring maximum or disable backups silently to keep an estimate true.

The disposable experiment uses the supported Hobby defaults. The live `VolumeCreateInput` has no capacity argument, CLI `volume add` has no size flag, and shrinking is unsupported: request default 5 GB volumes, with no resize. The replica-limit input uses floating-point memory/vCPU fields, but type shape alone does not prove that any particular smaller limit is accepted. Avoid that assumption by budgeting the published Hobby ceiling of 8 GB and 8 vCPU for each single replica. [Volume sizes](https://docs.railway.com/volumes/reference), [replica ceilings and per-second prices](https://railway.com/pricing).

For the disposable proof only, propose at most four temporary service containers—two database targets, one bounded runner, and one verification app—each with exactly one replica, for **at most 30 minutes total from the first temporary running container to all containers stopped**. Prepare images and checks before this clock starts. At published per-second rates, four replicas consuming their full 8 GB/8 vCPU ceiling throughout this window cost approximately $0.667. At most three default 5 GB temporary volumes (including the restored replacement) plus a conservative 5 GB of exclusive canary-backup data, allowing 48 hours of deletion retention after the experiment, add approximately $0.210. At most 100 MB public verification egress adds $0.005. The modeled total is below $0.90 before tax; proposed one-off planning allowance is **$1 before tax**, within the existing Kikit/workspace limits and subject to approval. Use ordinary services, not Railway Sandboxes, which have separate pricing.

This is an operational resource/time bound, not a provider-enforced dollar cap. Stop and clean up if the 30-minute window is exhausted, a fourth temporary volume or second backup is required, a replica/resource ceiling is higher, or the modeled spend exceeds the allowance. No larger experiment, permanent second app/database, bucket, PITR or plan upgrade is proposed. Confirm each canary remains below the default volume's 2.5 GB manual-backup threshold. After recording results, remove the named disposable services, their temporary volumes and canary backup; verify removal and recheck lagged billed usage. Do not leave them running or preserve temporary storage while waiting for the second review. If cleanup cannot complete, report the continuing cost immediately. The actual production-backup drill needs its own exact resource/time estimate after the canary establishes a supported isolated path.

## Disposable proof, then the actual backup drill

1. Record exact running application commit, database/document/protocol versions, PostgreSQL image and active source deployment/volume IDs in the ignored worksheet. Recheck current usage, the unchanged hard limit and staged changes. The staged-change query can return an empty draft; inspect its contents, not merely its status. Do not commit unrelated pending work.
2. After explicit resource approval, create the two disposable database targets in the same project/environment and Amsterdam region, using default 5 GB volumes. Give them independent credentials, no public database endpoints, no production connection references, and exactly one replica each. Recheck the actual per-replica ceiling before starting. Use the source PostgreSQL version. Start the 30-minute temporary-resource clock with the first running canary container; seed only synthetic account/session, private/shared note, binary snapshot/tail, independent receipt and deletion records. Do not copy real notes into test artifacts.
3. Confirm the canary's manual-backup size eligibility, trigger a labeled backup and record completion, timestamp and expiry. Alter only canary data after that backup so the restored boundary is distinguishable. Start the measured recovery clock when restoration begins.
4. Restore the **canary** backup, leaving its replacement mount staged. Inspect the complete diff. Test whether the newly restored volume can be assigned to the second canary while discarding the source replacement/detachment changes. Before applying anything, require the diff to affect only the isolated destination; retain the original canary volume and running deployment. Applying a staged patch redeploys every affected service, so a production app/database change in this diff is a stop condition. [Staged changes](https://docs.railway.com/deployments/staged-changes).
5. Apply only the verified isolated destination patch. Confirm the source service kept its original mounted volume and deployment and its post-backup changes remain. Confirm the isolated destination contains the earlier backup state. If this handoff is unsupported or mutates the source, leave production untouched and record the blocked boundary; a canary restore alone cannot close MAC-102.
6. Record the canary outcome and clean up its named temporary resources within the 30-minute bound, even if proof is incomplete. Only after this mechanism passes, prepare a second concrete review for the **actual production backup**: source backup/volume IDs, unchanged production mounts and deployments, isolated destination, expected staged diff, costs and rollback. A fresh logical copy or a canary backup must not be substituted for that actual backup. Do not use optional replica/wipe restore arguments. No production restore or cutover is authorized by this document.
7. Once the actual isolated path and cost are approved, enable only the daily schedule on the existing production volume. Confirm the returned daily kind and six-day retention; record the first completed scheduled backup and a later completed run with its interval. Alert/inspect failed or stale backups: a configured schedule alone cannot establish the 24-hour target.
8. Restore a completed production backup through the proven isolated procedure and run the checks below against synthetic records only. Leave the active production application/database and volume intact. Record elapsed recovery time and actual resource use. Clean up only the explicitly named temporary resources after recording evidence; confirm no running temporary containers, restored volumes or manual backup residue continue billing.

## Evidence required before closing the gate

Use compatible application assets and a separately configured runtime role. Record the exact backup boundary and hashes/identities for selected synthetic data without logging tokens or note contents. Check accounts, authorization and runtime DDL denial; private-page denial and owner/editor permissions; binary initial state, snapshots and tail; original normal/repaired receipt replay after compaction; continued edits and acknowledgement; retained browser pending identities through reconnect; and a deleted identity refusing recreation.

Keep the restored database isolated while reconciling known post-backup deletions, membership revocations and invitation invalidations. A backup may restore older permissions and sessions along with content; reopening it requires a reviewed session/access reconciliation procedure. If the surviving source cannot establish those changes, report that uncertainty rather than assuming restored permissions are current. The accepted recovery window permits loss of newer server records; it does not establish a verified reopening procedure.

Record recovery-start, database-ready and verified-app-ready timestamps, backup age, measured data loss, source-preservation checks and actual cost. Compare results with the 24-hour/four-hour targets under the recorded conditions. The [local logical restore](deployment.md#backups-and-restore), application Yjs snapshots and provider volume snapshots prove different boundaries. None alone establishes a hosted availability guarantee.
